SECTION 1: WATER OF CRYSTALLISATION
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Many salts contain water molecules trapped within their crystal structure.
These are called HYDRATED salts. The water is called "water of crystallisation."
When heated, the water is driven off, leaving the ANHYDROUS salt.

Example: CuSO4.5H2O (hydrated copper(II) sulfate) -> CuSO4 (anhydrous) + 5H2O
- Hydrated: blue crystals
- Anhydrous: white powder
- Adding water to anhydrous CuSO4 turns it blue again (test for water).

CALCULATION METHOD:
  1. Heat the hydrated salt to constant mass (heat, cool, weigh, repeat until
     mass no longer changes).
  2. Mass of water lost = initial mass - final mass.
  3. Mass of anhydrous salt = final mass.
  4. Moles of anhydrous salt = mass / Mr(anhydrous).
  5. Moles of water = mass of water / Mr(H2O) = mass of water / 18.
  6. Mole ratio = moles of water / moles of anhydrous salt = x.

WORKED EXAMPLE:
  11.1 g of hydrated copper(II) sulfate, CuSO4.xH2O, was heated to a
  constant mass of 7.09 g. Find x and the formula of the hydrated salt.
  (Mr: Cu = 63.5, S = 32.1, O = 16, H = 1)

Step 1: Mass of water lost
  Mass of water = 11.1 - 7.09 = 4.01 g

Step 2: Moles of anhydrous CuSO4
  Mr(CuSO4) = 63.5 + 32.1 + (4 x 16) = 159.6
  Moles of CuSO4 = 7.09 / 159.6 = 0.0444 mol

Step 3: Moles of water
  Mr(H2O) = (2 x 1) + 16 = 18
  Moles of H2O = 4.01 / 18 = 0.2228 mol

Step 4: Mole ratio
  x = moles of H2O / moles of CuSO4 = 0.2228 / 0.0444 = 5.02 ≈ 5

Step 5: Formula
  CuSO4.5H2O

NOTE: The small discrepancy (5.02 instead of exactly 5) is due to
experimental error (e.g. incomplete heating, water reabsorbed from air).
Round to the nearest whole number.

EXPERIMENTAL PROCEDURE TO DETERMINE x:
  1. Weigh an empty boiling tube. Record mass (m1).
  2. Add hydrated salt. Weigh and record mass (m2). Mass of hydrated salt = m2 - m1.
  3. Heat gently with a Bunsen burner, holding the tube at an angle.
  4. Allow to cool. Weigh and record mass (m3).
  5. Reheat, cool, and weigh again. Repeat until the mass is constant
     (no further change = all water has been driven off). Record final mass (m3).
  6. Mass of anhydrous salt = m3 - m1.
  7. Mass of water = (m2 - m1) - (m3 - m1) = m2 - m3.
  8. Calculate moles and find x.

CRITICAL: Heat GENTLY. Too strong heating can decompose the salt (e.g. CuSO4
may break down further if overheated). Weigh to constant mass to ensure all
water is removed.

SECTION 2: PERCENTAGE YIELD
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Yield = the amount of product obtained from a reaction.

Percentage yield = (actual yield / theoretical yield) x 100

Where:
  - Actual yield = the measured mass of product obtained in the experiment.
  - Theoretical yield = the calculated maximum mass of product possible,
    based on the balanced equation and the amount of limiting reactant.

The theoretical yield assumes 100% conversion (no losses).

WORKED EXAMPLE:
  In a reaction, 37 g of product was obtained. The theoretical yield was
  calculated to be 100 g. What is the percentage yield?

  Percentage yield = (37 / 100) x 100 = 37%

WORKED EXAMPLE (with moles):
  10.0 g of magnesium reacts with excess oxygen. 12.5 g of magnesium oxide
  was obtained. Calculate the percentage yield.
  (Mr: Mg = 24, O = 16)

Step 1: Equation
  2Mg + O2 -> 2MgO

Step 2: Moles of Mg (limiting reactant)
  Moles of Mg = 10.0 / 24 = 0.4167 mol

Step 3: Theoretical moles of MgO
  Mole ratio Mg:MgO = 1:1
  Moles of MgO = 0.4167 mol

Step 4: Theoretical mass of MgO
  Mr(MgO) = 24 + 16 = 40
  Theoretical mass = 0.4167 x 40 = 16.67 g

Step 5: Percentage yield
  Percentage yield = (12.5 / 16.67) x 100 = 75.0%

WHY IS YIELD USUALLY LESS THAN 100%?
  - Incomplete reaction (equilibrium not reached).
  - Side reactions producing other products.
  - Loss of product during transfer (spillage, residue on apparatus).
  - Product left dissolved in solution (not fully crystallised).
  - Impurities in reactants.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
SECTION 3: PERCENTAGE PURITY
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Percentage purity = (mass of pure substance / total mass of impure sample) x 100

WORKED EXAMPLE:
  An impure sample of limestone (CaCO3) has a mass of 10.0 g. When reacted
  with excess acid, 2.20 g of CO2 was produced. Calculate the percentage
  purity of CaCO3.
  (Mr: Ca = 40, C = 12, O = 16)

Step 1: Equation
  CaCO3 + 2HCl -> CaCl2 + H2O + CO2

Step 2: Moles of CO2 produced
  Mr(CO2) = 12 + (2 x 16) = 44
  Moles of CO2 = 2.20 / 44 = 0.0500 mol

Step 3: Mole ratio -> moles of CaCO3
  CaCO3 : CO2 = 1 : 1
  Moles of CaCO3 = 0.0500 mol

Step 4: Mass of pure CaCO3
  Mr(CaCO3) = 40 + 12 + (3 x 16) = 100
  Mass of pure CaCO3 = 0.0500 x 100 = 5.00 g

Step 5: Percentage purity
  Percentage purity = (5.00 / 10.0) x 100 = 50.0%

NOTE: The impurities do not react with the acid. Only the pure CaCO3 produces
CO2. By measuring the gas produced, you can calculate how much pure CaCO3 was
present.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
SECTION 4: COMMON CALCULATION MISTAKES
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

1. Not writing the balanced equation before calculating moles. The mole ratio
   comes from the equation.

2. Forgetting to multiply by 100 to convert to a percentage.

3. Using actual yield and theoretical yield in the wrong positions. Actual
   yield is always less than or equal to theoretical yield.

4. For water of crystallisation: not heating to constant mass. If the mass
   is still decreasing, water is still being lost.

5. Rounding too early. Carry all decimal places through and round only at the
   final answer.

6. Confusing percentage yield with percentage purity. Yield compares actual
   vs theoretical product. Purity compares pure mass vs total mass.

7. Using the wrong Mr. Double-check all relative atomic masses.
