SECTION 1: SOLUBILITY RULES TABLE
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

SALT TYPE         | SOLUBILITY      | INSOLUBLE EXCEPTIONS
------------------+-----------------+------------------------------------------
Group I salts     | ALL soluble     | None
(Li+, Na+, K+)    |                 |
Ammonium (NH4+)   | ALL soluble     | None
Nitrates (NO3-)   | ALL soluble     | None
Chlorides (Cl-)   | Most soluble    | AgCl, PbCl2
Bromides (Br-)    | Most soluble    | AgBr, PbBr2
Iodides (I-)      | Most soluble    | AgI, PbI2
Sulfates (SO4 2-) | Most soluble    | BaSO4, PbSO4, CaSO4 (slightly)
Carbonates (CO3 2-)| Mostly insoluble| Soluble if Group I or NH4+
Hydroxides (OH-)  | Mostly insoluble| Soluble if Group I, Ba(OH)2, Ca(OH)2 (slightly)
Oxides (O 2-)     | Mostly insoluble| Soluble if Group I or NH4+
Phosphates (PO4 3-)| Mostly insoluble| Soluble if Group I or NH4+
Sulfides (S 2-)   | Mostly insoluble| Soluble if Group I, Group II, NH4+

MEMORY AIDS:
- "SPA" salts (Sodium, Potassium, Ammonium) are ALWAYS soluble — no exceptions.
- All NITRATES are soluble — no exceptions.
- Chlorides: exceptions are Silver and Lead ("Smart Learners" = S, L).
- Sulfates: exceptions are Barium, Calcium, Lead ("Big Cats Like milk" = B, C, L).
- Most carbonates and hydroxides are insoluble except SPA + calcium hydroxide.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
SECTION 2: SALT PREPARATION — CHOOSING THE METHOD
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

DECISION TREE:
1. Is the salt SOLUBLE?
   - NO  -> Use PRECIPITATION (Method A)
   - YES -> Continue to step 2

2. Is the salt an SPA salt (Sodium, Potassium, or Ammonium)?
   - YES -> Use TITRATION (Method C)
   - NO  -> Use EXCESS INSOLUBLE BASE (Method B)

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
SECTION 3: METHOD A — PRECIPITATION (for insoluble salts)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Use this when the salt is insoluble in water (e.g. lead(II) sulfate, barium
sulfate, silver chloride, calcium carbonate).

PRINCIPLE: Mix two soluble solutions — one containing the cation, the other
containing the anion. The insoluble salt precipitates out.

EXAMPLE: Prepare lead(II) sulfate, PbSO4
- Mix lead(II) nitrate solution + sodium sulfate solution
- Pb2+(aq) + SO4 2-(aq) -> PbSO4(s)

STEPS:
1. Mix two soluble solutions together (one with the cation, one with the anion).
2. A solid (precipitate) forms immediately.
3. Filter the mixture to collect the solid residue.
4. Wash the residue with a little distilled water to remove impurities.
5. Dry the salt between sheets of filter paper.

NOTE: The other ions (e.g. Na+ and NO3-) remain in solution (the filtrate)
because their salts are soluble. They do not interfere.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
SECTION 4: METHOD B — EXCESS INSOLUBLE BASE (for soluble non-SPA salts)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Use this for soluble salts that are NOT SPA salts (e.g. copper(II) sulfate,
magnesium chloride, zinc nitrate).

PRINCIPLE: React an acid with an EXCESS of an insoluble solid (metal, metal
oxide, metal hydroxide, or metal carbonate). The excess is removed by
filtration. Using excess ensures all the acid is used up (no acid contaminates
the salt).

EXAMPLE: Prepare copper(II) sulfate from sulfuric acid + copper(II) oxide
- H2SO4(aq) + CuO(s) -> CuSO4(aq) + H2O(l)

STEPS:
1. Add the insoluble solid (e.g. CuO) to the acid in a beaker.
2. Stir and keep adding until no more solid dissolves (excess).
   - This ensures all the acid has reacted.
3. Filter to remove the unreacted solid (residue).
   - The filtrate contains the salt solution.
4. Heat the filtrate to evaporate some water (concentration).
   - Test for saturation: dip a clean cold glass rod into the solution;
     if crystals form on the rod, it is saturated.
5. Allow the saturated solution to cool slowly so crystals form.
6. Filter the crystals, wash with a small amount of cold distilled water,
   and dry between sheets of filter paper.

WHY EXCESS? Because both reactants and the product are soluble, we cannot
separate excess reactant by filtration. Using an INSOLUBLE solid means the
excess can be filtered off. The acid is fully consumed.

NOTE: For metals, use moderately reactive metals (e.g. zinc, magnesium).
Do NOT use very reactive metals (e.g. sodium, potassium) — dangerous.
Do NOT use unreactive metals (e.g. copper, silver) — no reaction with dilute
acid (copper does not react with dilute HCl).

EVAPORATION vs CRYSTALLISATION:
- Evaporation to dryness: heat until all water is gone. Use only if the
  salt does NOT decompose on heating (e.g. NaCl).
- Crystallisation: heat to concentrate, then cool. Use if the salt MAY
  decompose on strong heating (e.g. CuSO4, FeSO4). This is the safer method.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
SECTION 5: METHOD C — TITRATION (for soluble SPA salts)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Use this for soluble Sodium, Potassium, or Ammonium salts (e.g. NaCl, KNO3,
NH4Cl). Both reactants AND the product are all soluble, so the excess method
cannot be used. Titration allows exact stoichiometric amounts with no excess.

EXAMPLE: Prepare sodium chloride from HCl + NaOH
- HCl(aq) + NaOH(aq) -> NaCl(aq) + H2O(l)

STEPS:
1. Use a pipette to measure a fixed volume (e.g. 25.0 cm3) of alkali into
   a conical flask.
2. Add 2-3 drops of indicator (e.g. methyl orange).
3. Fill a burette with the acid.
4. Add acid from the burette while swirling the flask, until the indicator
   changes colour permanently (endpoint).
5. Record the volume of acid used (titre = final - initial burette reading).
6. REPEAT the titration without indicator, using the exact volumes found.
   - The indicator is removed so it does not contaminate the pure salt.
7. Evaporate the solution to obtain the salt.
   - For NaCl: evaporate to dryness (NaCl does not decompose).

WHY TITRATION? Because both reactants are soluble, you cannot add excess and
filter it off. You need exact volumes so there is no unreacted acid or alkali
left in the product.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
SECTION 6: TYPES OF ACID REACTIONS FOR SALT MAKING
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Acid + Metal      -> Salt + Hydrogen gas
  e.g. H2SO4 + Zn  -> ZnSO4 + H2
  (Use moderately reactive metals: Zn, Mg, Fe. Not Cu, Ag, Au. Not Na, K.)

Acid + Base (insoluble oxide/hydroxide) -> Salt + Water
  e.g. 2HCl + CuO -> CuCl2 + H2O
  e.g. H2SO4 + CuO -> CuSO4 + H2O

Acid + Carbonate  -> Salt + Water + Carbon dioxide
  e.g. 2HCl + Na2CO3 -> 2NaCl + H2O + CO2
  e.g. H2SO4 + CuCO3 -> CuSO4 + H2O + CO2

Acid + Alkali (soluble base) -> Salt + Water (neutralisation)
  e.g. HCl + NaOH -> NaCl + H2O
  e.g. H2SO4 + 2NaOH -> Na2SO4 + 2H2O

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
SECTION 7: COMMON ERRORS IN SALT PREPARATION
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

1. Using the excess method for an SPA salt. If both reactants are soluble,
   you cannot filter off excess. You MUST use titration.

2. Forgetting to remove the indicator in the titration method. The indicator
   contaminates the salt. Repeat without indicator.

3. Evaporating to dryness when the salt decomposes on heating. Copper(II)
   sulfate and iron(II) sulfate should be crystallised, not evaporated to
   dryness.

4. Using a very reactive metal (Na, K) with acid — dangerous and impractical.

5. Using copper metal with dilute HCl — no reaction (copper is below hydrogen
   in the reactivity series).

6. Not washing crystals. Wash with a small amount of COLD distilled water
   to remove soluble impurities without dissolving too much product.

7. Washing with a large amount of hot water — this dissolves and loses the
   product. Use a small amount of cold water.
