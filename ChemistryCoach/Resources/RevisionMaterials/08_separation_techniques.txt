SEPARATION TECHNIQUES — COMPLETE REFERENCE

The choice of separation method depends on the physical properties of the
substances in the mixture — usually differences in solubility, boiling point,
density, or sublimability.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
SECTION 2: DETAILED DESCRIPTIONS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

1. FILTRATION
   - Used to separate an insoluble solid from a liquid (e.g. sand from water).
   - Pour mixture through filter paper in a funnel.
   - RESIDUE = solid left on the filter paper.
   - FILTRATE = liquid that passes through.
   - Can also use centrifugation for very fine solids.

2. CRYSTALLISATION
   - Used to separate a dissolved solid from a solution when the solid is
     more soluble in hot solvent than cold.
   - Heat the solution to evaporate some solvent (concentration).
   - Test for saturation: dip a clean cold glass rod; if crystals form on
     removal, the solution is saturated.
   - Allow to cool slowly. Crystals form as solubility decreases.
   - Filter crystals, wash with cold water, dry between filter paper.
   - Use when the solid might decompose on strong heating.

3. EVAPORATION TO DRYNESS
   - Used when the solid does NOT decompose on heating (e.g. NaCl).
   - Heat the solution until all solvent is evaporated.
   - The solid remains as a dry powder.
   - NOT suitable for salts that decompose (e.g. CuSO4, FeSO4).

4. SIMPLE DISTILLATION
   - Used to separate a pure liquid from a solution (e.g. pure water from
     saltwater).
   - Solution is heated; the liquid with the lowest boiling point vaporises.
   - Vapour passes through a condenser (cold water jacket), condenses back
     to liquid, and is collected.
   - The solid solute remains in the flask.
   - The thermometer bulb should sit level with the side-arm of the flask.
     It measures the temperature of the VAPOUR passing that point, which is
     used to infer the boiling point of the liquid currently distilling
     over (it is not directly "the boiling point of the distillate" — it
     is the vapour temperature at the thermometer bulb).

5. FRACTIONAL DISTILLATION
   - Used to separate two or more miscible liquids with different boiling
     points (e.g. ethanol and water).
   - A fractionating column is used (with glass beads or plates) to provide
     multiple condensation-evaporation cycles.
   - The liquid with the lowest boiling point distils off first.
   - Example: ethanol (bp 78C) distils before water (bp 100C).
   - The thermometer reading stays close to 78C while mostly ethanol is
     distilling over, then rises as the mixture becomes richer in water
     (for a real ethanol-water mixture the vapour composition changes
     gradually rather than switching at a single fixed temperature, so
     "78C" is an approximate guide, not a rigid cut-off).
   - Collect the fraction distilling at/near 78C separately; heating is
     usually stopped once the temperature has clearly risen well past 78C
     and is approaching 100C, or once the desired fraction has been
     collected.
   - Used industrially: fractional distillation of crude oil.

6. SEPARATING FUNNEL
   - Used to separate immiscible liquids (e.g. oil and water).
   - Pour mixture into a separating funnel.
   - Allow layers to form (denser liquid sinks to the bottom).
   - Open the tap to drain the lower layer, then close before the interface.
   - The upper layer can be poured out from the top.

7. SUBLIMATION
   - Used when one substance sublimes (turns directly from solid to gas).
   - Heat the mixture. The sublimable solid turns to gas, leaving the other
     substance behind.
   - The gas can be cooled and collected on a cold surface.
   - Sublimable solids: iodine, naphthalene, ammonium chloride, dry ice.

8. PAPER CHROMATOGRAPHY
   - Used to separate dissolved substances (e.g. dyes in food colouring, inks).
   - Draw a pencil line (baseline) near the bottom of chromatography paper.
   - Place a small spot of the mixture on the baseline.
   - Suspend the paper in a solvent (e.g. water, ethanol) so that the solvent
     level is BELOW the baseline.
   - The solvent rises up the paper by capillary action.
   - Different substances travel different distances based on their solubility
     in the solvent.
   - Remove the paper when the solvent front nears the top. Mark the solvent
     front.
   - Calculate Rf value: Rf = distance travelled by substance / distance
     travelled by solvent front.
   - Compare Rf values with known standards to identify substances.

   Rf value is a constant for a given substance under fixed conditions
   (same solvent, same temperature). Rf ranges from 0 to 1.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
SECTION 3: CHOOSING THE RIGHT METHOD — DECISION GUIDE
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

What are you separating?
  - Solid + liquid (insoluble solid):
    -> Filtration or centrifugation

  - Solid + liquid (dissolved solid):
    -> If solid decomposes on heating: CRYSTALLISATION
    -> If solid does NOT decompose: EVAPORATION TO DRYNESS

  - Two miscible liquids (different boiling points):
    -> If boiling points are far apart: SIMPLE DISTILLATION
    -> If boiling points are close together: FRACTIONAL DISTILLATION

  - Two immiscible liquids:
    -> SEPARATING FUNNEL

  - Solid that sublimes + solid that does not:
    -> SUBLIMATION

  - Mixture of dissolved dyes/substances:
    -> PAPER CHROMATOGRAPHY

  - Mixture of solids with different solubilities:
    -> Dissolve in a suitable solvent, then filter/crystallise.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
SECTION 4: COMMON MISTAKES
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

1. Using evaporation to dryness for a salt that decomposes. Copper(II)
   sulfate turns from blue to white to black if overheated. Use
   crystallisation instead.

2. Using simple distillation when fractional distillation is needed. If two
   miscible liquids have close boiling points, simple distillation will not
   separate them effectively.

3. Drawing the baseline in pen instead of pencil. Pen ink will dissolve in
   the solvent and interfere with the chromatography.

4. Placing the solvent level above the baseline in chromatography. The spots
   will dissolve into the solvent instead of travelling up the paper.

5. Not marking the solvent front before it evaporates. You need the solvent
   front distance to calculate Rf values.

6. Forgetting to wash crystals after filtration. Impurities remain on the
   surface. Wash with a small amount of COLD solvent.

7. Not recording the boiling point during distillation. The thermometer
   reading confirms the identity and purity of the distillate.
