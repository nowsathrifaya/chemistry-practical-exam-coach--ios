--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
SECTION 1: KEY CONCEPTS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Electrolysis = decomposition of an electrolyte by electricity.

- ELECTROLYTE: A substance that conducts electricity when molten or in
  aqueous solution, and is decomposed by it. Electrolytes contain free-moving
  ions.

- ANODE (+): Positive electrode. Connected to the positive terminal of the
  battery. Anions (negative ions) are attracted here. OXIDATION occurs
  (loss of electrons).

- CATHODE (-): Negative electrode. Connected to the negative terminal.
  Cations (positive ions) are attracted here. REDUCTION occurs
  (gain of electrons).

MEMORY AID: "CAThode attracts CAT-ions" (cathode = negative, attracts + ions)
            "Anion" = "AN-ION" (anions go to anode)
            "OIL RIG": Oxidation Is Loss, Reduction Is Gain (of electrons)
            "Red Cat": REDuction occurs at the CAThode

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
SECTION 2: MOLTEN ELECTROLYTES
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

When a pure ionic compound is melted, only its own ions are present.
Predicting products is straightforward.

EXAMPLE: Electrolysis of molten lead(II) bromide (PbBr2)

  | Electrode  | Ion discharged | Product              | Half-equation
  |------------+-----------------+----------------------+--------------------
  | Cathode (-)| Pb2+            | Lead metal (grey)    | Pb2+ + 2e- -> Pb
  | Anode (+)  | Br-             | Bromine (brown gas)  | 2Br- -> Br2 + 2e-

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
SECTION 3: AQUEOUS ELECTROLYTES — SELECTIVE DISCHARGE
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

When an ionic compound is dissolved in water, H+ and OH- ions from water are
also present. At each electrode, competing ions exist. Only one is
preferentially discharged.

AT THE CATHODE (reduction):
  - If the metal is BELOW hydrogen in the reactivity series (less reactive,
    e.g. Cu, Ag, Au):
    -> The METAL ION is discharged (e.g. Cu2+ + 2e- -> Cu)
  - If the metal is ABOVE hydrogen in the reactivity series (more reactive,
    e.g. Na, K, Ca, Mg, Al):
    -> HYDROGEN is discharged instead (2H+ + 2e- -> H2)
  - If the metal is near hydrogen (e.g. Zn, Fe):
    -> HYDROGEN is usually discharged (rule of thumb at O-Level)

AT THE ANODE (oxidation) — the O-LEVEL EXAM RULE (a simplified selective
discharge rule, not a universal law of electrochemistry):
  - If HALIDE ions (Cl-, Br-, I-) are present in HIGH concentration:
    -> The HALIDE is discharged (e.g. 2Cl- -> Cl2 + 2e-)
  - Otherwise (no halide, or dilute halide):
    -> OXYGEN is discharged from OH- (4OH- -> O2 + 2H2O + 4e-)
  This "concentrated halide -> halogen, dilute halide -> oxygen" rule is the
  version examiners expect at O-Level. It is a useful approximation, not a
  strict universal rule — treat it as the exam-level rule to apply here.

REACTIVITY SERIES (relevant part):
  K, Na, Ca, Mg, Al, [Zn, Fe], H, Cu, Ag, Au
  <-- more reactive                        less reactive -->

  Metals LEFT of H: hydrogen discharged at cathode
  Metals RIGHT of H: metal discharged at cathode

SECTION 4: COMMON ELECTROLYSIS EXAMPLES
================================================================================

EXAMPLE 1: Electrolysis of aqueous copper(II) sulfate (CuSO4) with inert
electrodes (e.g. carbon/platinum)

  Ions present: Cu2+, SO4 2-, H+, OH-

  Cathode: Cu2+ is below hydrogen -> Cu discharged
    Cu2+(aq) + 2e- -> Cu(s)
    Observation: Reddish-brown deposit on cathode.

  Anode: No halide present -> OH- discharged -> O2
    4OH-(aq) -> O2(g) + 2H2O(l) + 4e-
    Observation: Bubbles of colourless gas (relights glowing splint).

  Overall: Blue colour of solution fades (Cu2+ removed). H+ builds up,
  solution becomes acidic (H2SO4 remains).

EXAMPLE 2: Electrolysis of dilute sodium chloride solution (NaCl)

  Ions present: Na+, Cl-, H+, OH-

  Cathode: Na+ is above hydrogen -> H2 discharged
    2H+(aq) + 2e- -> H2(g)
    Observation: Bubbles of colourless gas (pop with lighted splint).

  Anode: Cl- is dilute -> OH- discharged -> O2
    4OH-(aq) -> O2(g) + 2H2O(l) + 4e-
    Observation: Bubbles of colourless gas (relights glowing splint).

EXAMPLE 3: Electrolysis of CONCENTRATED sodium chloride solution (brine)

  Cathode: Same as above -> H2 discharged
    2H+(aq) + 2e- -> H2(g)

  Anode: Cl- is in HIGH concentration -> Cl2 discharged
    2Cl-(aq) -> Cl2(g) + 2e-
    Observation: Greenish-yellow gas (bleaches damp blue litmus).

  Overall: Na+ and OH- remain -> sodium hydroxide forms. This is the
  industrial chlor-alkali process.

EXAMPLE 4: Electrolysis of dilute sulfuric acid (H2SO4)

  Ions present: H+, SO4 2-, OH-

  Cathode: H+ discharged
    2H+(aq) + 2e- -> H2(g)

  Anode: No halide -> OH- discharged
    4OH-(aq) -> O2(g) + 2H2O(l) + 4e-

  Overall: Water is decomposed. H2SO4 becomes more concentrated (water
  removed, acid remains).

EXAMPLE 5: Electrolysis of molten aluminium oxide (Al2O3) — industrial

  Cathode: Al3+ + 3e- -> Al

  Theoretical (inert electrode) anode half-equation:
  Anode: 2O2- -> O2 + 4e-

  ACTUAL Hall-Heroult process: the anodes are made of CARBON (graphite),
  not an inert material. The oxygen formed at the anode reacts with the
  hot carbon anode itself, so the anode is gradually consumed and must be
  replaced. The gas actually given off is mainly carbon dioxide (with
  some carbon monoxide), not oxygen gas:

  O2 + C -> CO2   (main reaction; some 2O2 + C -> ... side reactions also
                    give a smaller amount of CO)

  So for an EXAM ANSWER about the half-equations, "2O2- -> O2 + 4e-" is
  the correct half-equation to write (it shows electron transfer at the
  anode). But if asked to describe the actual INDUSTRIAL PROCESS, you
  should also state that the anodes are carbon and are consumed, forming
  mainly CO2 rather than escaping as O2 gas.

  Used in the Hall-Heroult process. Cryolite is added to lower the melting
  point from ~2000C to ~950C.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
SECTION 5: WRITING HALF-EQUATIONS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

RULES:
1. Balance the atoms first.
2. Balance the charge by adding electrons.
3. Electrons go on the LEFT for reduction (cathode).
4. Electrons go on the RIGHT for oxidation (anode).

Cathode (reduction) examples:
  Cu2+(aq) + 2e- -> Cu(s)
  Pb2+(aq) + 2e- -> Pb(s)
  Ag+(aq) + e- -> Ag(s)
  Al3+(aq) + 3e- -> Al(s)
  2H+(aq) + 2e- -> H2(g)

Anode (oxidation) examples:
  2Cl-(aq) -> Cl2(g) + 2e-
  2Br-(aq) -> Br2(l) + 2e-
  2I-(aq) -> I2(s) + 2e-
  4OH-(aq) -> O2(g) + 2H2O(l) + 4e-

CHECK: The number of electrons must match the charge on the ion.
  Cu2+ needs 2 electrons. Al3+ needs 3 electrons.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
SECTION 6: COMMON ELECTROLYSIS MISTAKES
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

1. Reversing anode/cathode. Anode = positive = oxidation. Cathode =
   negative = reduction. Do NOT reverse these.

2. Forgetting that water provides H+ and OH- ions in aqueous solutions.
   These compete with the dissolved ions.

3. Saying "oxygen is discharged at the anode" without explaining it comes
   from OH- ions. Show the half-equation.

4. Not balancing electrons in half-equations. Each side must have the same
   total charge.

5. Confusing "concentrated" and "dilute" for NaCl electrolysis. Concentrated
   -> Cl2 at anode. Dilute -> O2 at anode. This is the most tested distinction.

6. Saying the solution becomes more alkaline after CuSO4 electrolysis. It
   becomes more ACIDIC (H+ and SO4 2- remain, water is consumed, Cu2+
   depleted).

7. Not stating observations. "Copper is deposited" is correct but incomplete.
   Say "reddish-brown solid deposited on the cathode."
