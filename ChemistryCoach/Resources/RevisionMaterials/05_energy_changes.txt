SECTION 1: EXOTHERMIC vs ENDOTHERMIC REACTIONS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

EXOTHERMIC REACTION:
  - Heat is RELEASED to the surroundings.
  - Temperature of surroundings INCREASES.
  - Energy of products < energy of reactants.
  - Delta H is NEGATIVE (-).
  - Examples: combustion, neutralisation, respiration, rusting.

ENDOTHERMIC REACTION:
  - Heat is ABSORBED from the surroundings.
  - Temperature of surroundings DECREASES.
  - Energy of products > energy of reactants.
  - Delta H is POSITIVE (+).
  - Examples: photosynthesis, thermal decomposition (e.g. CaCO3 -> CaO + CO2),
    dissolving ammonium nitrate in water.

MEMORY AID:
  "EX-othermic" = heat EXits the system
  "EN-dothermic" = heat enters INto the system

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
SECTION 2: BOND ENERGIES
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

GOLDEN RULES:
  - Bond BREAKING is ENDOTHERMIC (energy is absorbed/required).
  - Bond MAKING is EXOTHERMIC (energy is released).

Think of magnets: pulling them apart requires energy (breaking). Letting them
snap together releases energy (making).

To determine if a reaction is exothermic or endothermic:
  - If energy released (making bonds) > energy absorbed (breaking bonds):
    EXOTHERMIC (net release of energy).
  - If energy absorbed (breaking bonds) > energy released (making bonds):
    ENDOTHERMIC (net absorption of energy).

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
SECTION 3: ENERGY LEVEL DIAGRAMS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

EXOTHERMIC DIAGRAM:
  - Reactants are HIGHER on the energy axis than products.
  - Energy "drops" from reactants to products (released).
  - Delta H arrow points DOWNWARD from reactants to products.

ENDOTHERMIC DIAGRAM:
  - Reactants are LOWER on the energy axis than products.
  - Energy "rises" from reactants to products (absorbed).
  - Delta H arrow points UPWARD from reactants to products.

ACTIVATION ENERGY (Ea):
  - The minimum energy that reactant particles must have to collide and react.
  - Shown as a "hump" from the reactant level to the peak of the curve.
  - A catalyst lowers the activation energy, providing an alternative pathway.

SECTION 4: CALORIMETRY — ENERGY TRANSFER CALCULATIONS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

FORMULA: q = m x c x Delta T

Where:
  q = energy transferred IN THIS PARTICULAR EXPERIMENT (J or kJ) — this is
      a total amount of energy, not "per mole".
  m = mass of the solution being heated (g) — assume 1 cm3 = 1 g
  c = specific heat capacity of water (4.2 J/g/C)
  Delta T = temperature change (T_final - T_initial) in C

  Delta H (enthalpy change) = q / (moles of limiting reactant). Delta H is
  an INTENSIVE quantity (per mole), whereas q is the total energy released
  or absorbed in that specific run of the experiment. Do not use the two
  terms interchangeably: q describes this one experiment; Delta H is the
  value per mole that you would quote as "the enthalpy change of
  neutralisation" and compare between experiments.

STEPS:
1. Measure initial temperature of the solution.
2. Add reactant, stir continuously.
3. Measure the maximum (or minimum) temperature reached.
4. Calculate Delta T = T_final - T_initial.
5. Calculate q = m x c x Delta T.
6. To find energy per mole: energy per mole = q / moles of limiting reactant.
7. Remember to include the correct SIGN: exothermic = negative, endothermic
   = positive.

WORKED EXAMPLE:
  50 cm3 of 1.00 mol/dm3 HCl is mixed with 50 cm3 of 1.00 mol/dm3 NaOH.
  The temperature rises from 22.0C to 28.5C. Calculate the enthalpy change
  of neutralisation per mole of water formed.

Step 1: Total mass of solution
  m = 50 + 50 = 100 g (assume 1 cm3 = 1 g)

Step 2: Temperature change
  Delta T = 28.5 - 22.0 = +6.5C (temperature rose -> exothermic)

Step 3: Energy transferred (q, for this experiment)
  q = m x c x Delta T = 100 x 4.2 x 6.5 = 2730 J = 2.73 kJ

Step 4: Moles of water formed
  HCl + NaOH -> NaCl + H2O
  Moles of HCl = 1.00 x 0.050 = 0.0500 mol
  Moles of NaOH = 1.00 x 0.050 = 0.0500 mol
  Mole ratio 1:1, so moles of H2O = 0.0500 mol

Step 5: Enthalpy change per mole (Delta H)
  Delta H = -2.73 / 0.0500 = -54.6 kJ/mol
  (Negative because the reaction is exothermic.)

Answer: -54.6 kJ/mol

NOTE: This is an experimentally-determined value calculated under the
simplifying assumptions above (no heat loss to the surroundings or to the
container, and that 1 cm3 of solution has the same specific heat capacity
as pure water). It is close to the accepted literature value for strong
acid-strong alkali neutralisation (about -57 kJ/mol) — small experimental
values like this should always be reported alongside the assumptions used,
not treated as an exact physical constant.

SECTION 5: CALORIMETRY — EXPERIMENTAL PROCEDURE
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

PLANNING A CALORIMETRY EXPERIMENT:
1. Measure a fixed volume of solution A (e.g. 50 cm3) using a measuring
   cylinder. Record the initial temperature.
2. Measure a fixed volume of solution B (e.g. 50 cm3). Record its initial
   temperature.
3. Pour solution B into solution A in a polystyrene cup (insulated to reduce
   heat loss).
4. Stir the mixture continuously and record the maximum temperature reached.
5. Calculate Delta T = T_max - T_initial.
6. Calculate q = m x c x Delta T (m = total mass of solution).
7. Calculate moles of limiting reactant.
8. Calculate Delta H = q / moles (with correct sign).

CONTROLS:
  - Same volumes of solutions each time.
  - Same initial temperature.
  - Same concentration of solutions.
  - Same type of cup (polystyrene, insulated).
  - Stirring at the same rate.

IMPROVING ACCURACY:
  - Use a polystyrene cup with a lid (reduces heat loss to surroundings).
  - Use a digital thermometer (more precise than a mercury thermometer).
  - Record the maximum temperature quickly before heat is lost.
  - Plot a cooling curve: take temperature readings every 30 seconds,
    extrapolate back to the time of mixing to find the true temperature change.

COMMON SOURCES OF ERROR:
  - Heat loss to the surroundings (largest error).
  - Heat absorbed by the cup itself.
  - Not all heat transferred to the solution (some heats the container).
  - Incomplete reaction.
  - Measurement error in temperature or volume.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
SECTION 6: COMMON ENERGY CHANGES MISTAKES
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

1. Forgetting the negative sign for exothermic reactions. Delta H must be
   negative for exothermic.

2. Using the mass of one solution instead of the total mass. If 50 cm3 of
   acid is mixed with 50 cm3 of alkali, the total mass is 100 g.

3. Not converting joules to kilojoules. q = m x c x Delta T gives joules.
   Divide by 1000 for kJ.

4. Using the wrong number of moles. Use the limiting reactant (the one that
   is fully consumed).

5. Confusing bond breaking and bond making. Breaking = endothermic (+).
   Making = exothermic (-).

6. Forgetting that activation energy is present in BOTH exothermic and
   endothermic reactions.
