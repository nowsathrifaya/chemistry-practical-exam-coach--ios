SECTION 1: FACTORS AFFECTING RATE
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Four factors affect the rate of a chemical reaction:
1. TEMPERATURE — higher temperature = faster (particles move faster, collide
   more often and with more energy).
2. CONCENTRATION — higher concentration = faster (more particles per unit
   volume, more frequent collisions).
3. SURFACE AREA / PARTICLE SIZE — smaller pieces = faster (more exposed
   surface for collisions).
4. CATALYST — presence of a catalyst = faster (provides alternative pathway
   with lower activation energy; catalyst is not consumed).

COLLISION THEORY:
  - Particles must collide to react.
  - Not all collisions lead to a reaction — only those with sufficient energy
    (activation energy) and correct orientation.
  - More frequent effective collisions = faster rate.

SECTION 2: MEASURING RATE OF REACTION
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

METHOD 1: Volume of gas produced over time
  - Collect gas in a gas syringe or by water displacement (inverted measuring
    cylinder).
  - Record volume of gas at regular time intervals (e.g. every 10 seconds).
  - Rate = volume of gas / time taken (cm3/s).
  - Graph: plot volume (y-axis) vs time (x-axis). Steeper initial gradient =
    faster rate.

METHOD 2: Time for a mark to disappear (precipitation)
  - Place a flask with a cross drawn on paper beneath it.
  - Add reactants; start timer.
  - Stop timer when the cross can no longer be seen through the solution.
  - Rate = 1 / time taken (s-1). Shorter time = faster rate.

METHOD 3: Mass loss over time
  - Place reactants in a conical flask on an electronic balance.
  - Record mass at regular intervals.
  - Rate = mass loss / time (g/s).
  - Note: only works if a gas escapes the system.

SECTION 3: PLANNING A RATE EXPERIMENT — FRAMEWORK
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Every planning answer must address:
(a) Independent Variable (IV) — what you change
(b) Dependent Variable (DV) — what you measure
(c) Controlled Variables (CVs) — what you keep the same

TYPE A: INVESTIGATING CONCENTRATION
  IV: Concentration of reactant (e.g. 0.5, 1.0, 1.5, 2.0, 2.5 mol/dm3)
  DV: Volume of gas produced after a fixed time (e.g. 60 seconds)
      OR time taken to collect a fixed volume of gas (e.g. 50 cm3)
  CVs: Volume of reactant, mass/size of solid reactant, temperature,
      same catalyst (if used), same apparatus.

TYPE B: INVESTIGATING TEMPERATURE
  IV: Temperature (e.g. 20, 30, 40, 50, 60C)
  DV: Time taken to collect a fixed volume of gas (e.g. 50 cm3)
      OR volume of gas collected in a fixed time (e.g. 60 seconds)
  CVs: Concentration, volume, mass/size of reactant, same catalyst.

TYPE C: INVESTIGATING PARTICLE SIZE / SURFACE AREA
  IV: Particle size (e.g. large lumps, small lumps, powder)
  DV: Volume of gas produced after a fixed time
  CVs: Mass of solid, concentration and volume of acid, temperature.

COMMON APPARATUS:
  - Electronic balance (0.01 g precision)
  - Measuring cylinder (to measure volume of solution)
  - Gas syringe (to collect gas) OR inverted measuring cylinder over water
  - Stopwatch (to measure time)
  - Conical flask (for reaction)
  - Thermometer (if temperature is a variable)
  - Bunsen burner or water bath (to heat solutions)

SECTION 4: SAMPLE PLANNING QUESTION (MODEL ANSWER)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

QUESTION:
  A student investigates how the concentration of hydrochloric acid affects
  the rate of reaction with magnesium. Plan an experiment.

ANSWER:

(a) Independent Variable: Concentration of HCl (e.g. 0.5, 1.0, 1.5, 2.0,
    2.5 mol/dm3)

(b) Dependent Variable: Volume of hydrogen gas produced in 60 seconds (cm3)

(c) Controlled Variables:
    - Volume of HCl used (e.g. 25 cm3)
    - Mass of magnesium (e.g. 0.50 g)
    - Surface area of magnesium (same size/shape)
    - Temperature (conduct at room temperature)
    - Same apparatus (gas syringe, conical flask)

(d) Procedure:
    1. Measure 25 cm3 of 0.5 mol/dm3 HCl using a measuring cylinder.
    2. Pour into a conical flask.
    3. Set up a gas syringe connected to the flask.
    4. Add 0.50 g of magnesium ribbon to the flask.
    5. Immediately start the stopwatch and insert the stopper.
    6. Record the volume of gas in the syringe after 60 seconds.
    7. Repeat with different concentrations (1.0, 1.5, 2.0, 2.5 mol/dm3),
       keeping all other variables constant.
    8. Repeat each concentration twice and calculate the mean volume.

(e) Safety: Wear goggles. Hydrogen gas is flammable — keep away from naked
    flames. Handle acid with care.

(f) Data treatment: Plot a graph of gas volume (y-axis) vs concentration
    (x-axis). A steeper increase indicates a faster rate.

(g) Evaluation: Ensure the stopper is inserted quickly to prevent gas
    escaping before the timer starts. Use a digital gas syringe for greater
    precision.

SECTION 5: RATE CALCULATIONS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

FORMULA: Rate = amount of product formed / time
         Rate = 1 / time (if measuring time for a fixed event)

WORKED EXAMPLE 1:
  In a reaction, 45 cm3 of CO2 was produced in 30 seconds.
  Rate = 45 / 30 = 1.5 cm3/s

WORKED EXAMPLE 2:
  A cross disappeared in 20 seconds.
  Rate = 1 / 20 = 0.050 s-1

WORKED EXAMPLE 3 (from graph):
  From a volume-time graph, the gradient at the start is steepest. To find
  the initial rate, calculate the gradient of the tangent at t=0:
  Rate = (change in volume) / (change in time) = (e.g. 60 cm3) / (15 s) = 4 cm3/s

KEY POINT: The rate is FASTEST at the start (steepest gradient) and SLOWS
DOWN over time (gradient decreases) as reactants are used up. The reaction
stops when one reactant is fully consumed (graph levels off).

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
SECTION 6: COMMON RATE EXPERIMENT MISTAKES
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

1. Using "amount" instead of "concentration" or "volume" in planning answers.
   Be specific: name the instrument (e.g. "measuring cylinder") and quantity.

2. Not listing enough controlled variables. Must include: volume, mass,
   temperature, particle size, and apparatus type.

3. Not repeating the experiment. State "repeat and calculate a mean."

4. Using a method that doesn't measure rate directly. For example, measuring
   the mass of solid before and after does not give a rate over time — you
   need readings at intervals.

5. Forgetting to start the timer at the same time as adding the reactant.

6. Losing gas before the syringe is connected. Insert the stopper quickly.

7. Not stating units for rate (cm3/s or s-1).

8. Confusing "rate = volume/time" with "rate = 1/time." Use 1/time only when
   measuring the time for a fixed event (e.g. cross disappearing). Use
   volume/time when measuring gas produced over a period.
